Where the architecture module left off

In the BigQuery module, clearmeet_analytics.qos_events was already full. 5 billion rows a day had to get there somehow.
  • Every participant’s client uploads a QoS sample every 10 seconds
  • ~6M meetings × ~4 participants × ~200 samples ≈ 5B messages/day
  • Weekday peak ≈ 90,000 messages/second; each message ≈ 350 bytes
  • Four different systems want those messages, for four different reasons
The architecture module started at the blue table. This module is the red question mark.

  • The four consumers (dashboard, alerting, BigQuery, archive) are the whole reason this is not just an HTTP endpoint writing rows — the next slide makes that concrete.

The naive version, and what breaks

  • First design: the participant clients POST their samples to a receiver service, which writes to BigQuery and calls the alerting service inline
  • Monday 9am: 90K samples/s arrive — and the alerting service is mid-redeploy
  • Every request now blocks on a dead dependency; the receiver backs up; clients retry; the retries make it worse
  • Adding a fourth consumer means changing the receiver again
The producer’s availability is the product of every consumer’s availability. That is the coupling a broker removes.
Speaker notes — The naive version
  • Ask the room how many have shipped this exact design. Most hands.
  • Three distinct couplings are failing here, and they map to the three decouplings on the next slide: time (the consumer must be up right now), rate (the consumer must be as fast as the producer), and space (the producer must know each consumer exists).

Put a message broker in the middle

A message broker is a service that accepts messages from producers and holds a copy for each consumer until that consumer has taken it. The clients publish straight to it — it is the ingestion point.
  • Decoupled in time: alerting can be down for its 4-minute redeploy; its messages wait for it
  • Decoupled in rate: samples keep arriving at 90K/s even though alerting only manages 20K/s. Alerting falls behind, then catches up
  • Decoupled in space: a publishing client names the broker and never a consumer. Adding the raw archive is a change to the broker’s configuration — no client is redeployed
Each consumer gets its own queue and its own position in it. One slow consumer no longer affects the others — or the producers.
Speaker notes — Put a message broker in the middle
  • “Message broker” is the generic industry term for this category of service — Pub/Sub, Apache Kafka, RabbitMQ, and Amazon SQS/SNS are all brokers. Define it here because the deck uses it throughout, and because section 5 compares two of them.
  • Say plainly that the broker replaces the ingest service from the previous slide. It is not something you put behind an ingestion tier — it is the ingestion endpoint, a global API the clients call directly.
  • Deliberately generic: no Pub/Sub vocabulary on this slide. Topics and subscriptions arrive on the next two slides, and students should already want them by then.
  • The differing queue depths are the single most important visual in the module. Everything in the “when it breaks” section is about reading those depths.

Speaker notes — Section 1: The model
  • Pulse-check: who has published to Pub/Sub before? Many hands → move quickly to slide 9 (the message-lifecycle diagram) and slow down there; it is the module’s map.

Four nouns

  • Publisher — anything that can call the API; ClearMeet’s clients publish through an edge service
  • Topic — a named resource messages are sent to. Not a queue; it holds nothing on its own
  • Subscription — the queue. Attached to one topic; holds unacknowledged messages; tracks what has been acked
  • Subscriber — the code that receives and acknowledges
A message published to a topic with zero subscriptions is dropped immediately. Create the subscription before the publisher starts.
The topic is a router. The subscriptions are the queues.
Speaker notes — Four nouns
  • The zero-subscription rule bites everyone once; it is the most common “where did my messages go” support question, and there is no error — the messages are simply gone. Make them write it down.
  • Warn against the mental model “the topic stores messages and subscribers read from it.” Kafka works closer to that; Pub/Sub does not. Section 5 makes the contrast explicit.

Publish and receive, in full

from google.cloud import pubsub_v1

publisher = pubsub_v1.PublisherClient()
topic_path = publisher.topic_path(
    "clearmeet-prod", "qos-samples")
publish_futures = []

for sample in batch_of_samples:
    data = json.dumps(sample).encode("utf-8")
    f = publisher.publish(topic_path, data,
                          device="web")
    f.add_done_callback(on_publish_done)
    publish_futures.append(f)

futures.wait(publish_futures,
             return_when=futures.ALL_COMPLETED)
python · subscriber
subscriber = pubsub_v1.SubscriberClient()
subscription_path = subscriber.subscription_path(
    "clearmeet-prod", "qos-dashboard-sub")

def callback(message):
    handle(json.loads(message.data))
    message.ack()

streaming_pull_future = subscriber.subscribe(
    subscription_path, callback=callback)

with subscriber:
    try:
        streaming_pull_future.result()
    except TimeoutError:
        streaming_pull_future.cancel()
        streaming_pull_future.result()
Both are asynchronous. publish() hands the message to a background batching thread and returns a future; subscribe() starts a background stream that calls your callback. The wait and the with block exist to make sure nothing is still in flight when the process exits.
Speaker notes — Publish and receive
  • This is the whole API. Everything after this slide is a setting on one of these two calls or on the subscription. Both snippets follow the handwritten samples in googleapis/python-pubsub/samples/snippets/ (publisher.py, subscriber.py) — the same repo the client defaults come from.
  • Warn them about the other sample family, because they will find it first. That repo also contains generated_samples/, produced automatically from the protobuf service definition, one file per RPC. Those look like client.publish(request=PublishRequest(topic=...)) and client.pull(request=PullRequest(...)). They demonstrate the raw API surface, not real usage — the generated publish sample sends no messages at all, and its pull is unary pull rather than StreamingPull. Production code uses the handwritten form on this slide.
  • The quickstart at docs.cloud.google.com/pubsub/docs/publish-receive-messages-client-library shows print(future.result()) inside the publish loop. That is fine for ten messages, but it blocks until the server confirms each one, so every message travels in a batch of one — which defeats the batching on the next slide. The collect-then-wait form here is what the same repo’s publish_messages_with_batch_settings sample uses.
  • The subscriber’s with subscriber: block closes the client cleanly; the TimeoutError path applies when result(timeout=...) is given a timeout, and cancel() then result() waits for the shutdown to finish.
  • Java equivalents: Publisher.newBuilder(topic).build() and Subscriber.newBuilder(subscription, receiver).build(); ApiFutures.addCallback(...) plays the role of add_done_callback.

Speaker notes — Section 2: Publishing

Batching depends on one publisher’s rate

The publisher client does not send each message immediately — it holds them briefly and sends a group in one request. The code is identical everywhere; what changes the outcome is how fast that one process calls publish().
publisher = pubsub_v1.PublisherClient(
  batch_settings=pubsub_v1.types
    .BatchSettings(
      max_messages=100,    # default
      max_bytes=1_000_000, # 1 MB
      max_latency=0.01,    # 10 ms
  )
)
  • Whichever limit is hit first sends the batch
  • Nothing waits longer than max_latency — the timer is a ceiling on delay, not a delay
Speaker notes — Batching depends on one publisher’s rate
  • Defaults verified in googleapis/python-pubsub, google/cloud/pubsub_v1/types.py: BatchSettings(max_bytes=1*1000*1000, max_latency=0.01, max_messages=100).
  • The point to land: a fleet total tells you nothing about batching. Batching happens inside one publisher client, so the only number that matters is that process’s own publish rate. Case A is the common misconception — people assume a high-volume system automatically benefits.
  • Arithmetic worth doing on the board, all from the defaults. At 45,000/s: one message every 1/45,000 s = 0.022 ms, so 100 messages take 2.22 ms and max_messages binds. At 450/s: one message every 2.22 ms, so in a 10 ms window only about 4 arrive and max_latency binds. At 1 message per 10 s (case A): the timer fires on a batch of one.
  • Head off the wrong conclusion about case A: nothing needs fixing there, and the client is not routed through some intermediate service. It publishes directly to the topic, exactly like case B — identical code and plumbing. It simply gets no batching benefit, and the 10 ms ceiling means it loses nothing either.
  • The tradeoff for a longer timer: buffered messages live in the publisher’s memory and are lost if the process dies before the batch is sent, which is why a shutdown path should flush.
  • The 1 MB byte limit protects you from the 10 MB publish-request cap when messages are large.

Data vs. attributes — and the limits

  • data is opaque bytes — Pub/Sub never parses it. Max 10 MB
  • attributes are string pairs the service can read: up to 100 per message; key ≤ 256 B, value ≤ 1024 B
  • Anything a subscription filter or routing decision needs must be an attribute, not a payload field
  • A topic can carry an Avro or Protobuf schema and reject non-conforming messages at publish time
Put in attributes only what the service needs to see. Attributes are delivered with every copy and count toward what you are billed for.
Speaker notes — Data vs. attributes
  • Limits from docs.cloud.google.com/pubsub/quotas — tagged service limit on the face so they read differently from the heuristics later in the deck. Also relevant: a publish request is capped at 10 MB and 1,000 messages total, which interacts with the batch settings.
  • The schema mention pays off on the BigQuery-subscription slide, where --use-topic-schema depends on the topic having one.

When arrival order changes the answer

ClearMeet’s session service consumes meeting-lifecycle and keeps a live roster: who is in meeting mtg_58d1 right now. It applies each event as it arrives — joined adds a name, left removes one.
  • Two events for amy: she joins at 14:00:09 and leaves at 14:31:02
  • Arrive in that order and the roster ends correct — amy removed
  • Arrive reversed and left removes a name that is not there yet, then joined adds her back. Amy is now in a meeting she has left, permanently
  • By default Pub/Sub makes no ordering promise — not even between two messages from the same publisher
Most streams do not care: a QoS sample carries its own timestamp, so late arrival changes nothing. A state machine driven by events is the case that cares.
Speaker notes — When arrival order changes the answer
  • Be concrete about why two messages can arrive reversed, since “the network reorders” is too vague to be useful. Three real mechanisms: (1) the join and the leave are published by different processes of the meeting-lifecycle service, each with its own batch buffer, so the later message can be flushed first; (2) the two publishes take different network paths and one is simply slower; (3) the join was redelivered — its ack deadline lapsed on the consumer — and the redelivery lands after the leave.
  • Note the vocabulary carefully: mechanism 3 is a redelivery, the term from the delivery section. Do not call it a publisher retry, which is the separate publish-side case.
  • Ask the room for the fix before revealing the next slide. Good answers: sort by timestamp in the consumer (works only if you can wait for stragglers), or make the consumer order-independent — here, storing a per-participant last-event timestamp and ignoring older events, which is genuinely the better fix when you can afford it. Ordering keys are the third option.
  • ClearMeet applies ordering to one topic only. The other three streams are order-independent by design.

Ordering keys: order within a conversation

publisher = pubsub_v1.PublisherClient(
  publisher_options=pubsub_v1.types
    .PublisherOptions(
      enable_message_ordering=True
  )                  # not the default
)
publisher.publish(topic, data,
    ordering_key="mtg_58d1")
  • Pub/Sub delivers messages carrying the same key in the order they were published. It promises nothing about two different keys, and does not need to — mtg_92aa’s events have no bearing on mtg_58d1’s roster
  • Turn it on in both places: the publisher, and the subscription at creation time (it cannot be added later)
  • Publish every message for one key from the same region
One roster per meeting, so one ordered lane per meeting.
Ordering caps publish throughput at 1 MB/s per key (service limit), so the key must divide the traffic finely. meeting_id gives millions of small lanes; keying on region would force a whole region through one 1 MB/s lane.
Speaker notes — Ordering keys
  • All conditions from docs.cloud.google.com/pubsub/docs/ordering. The same-region-on-publish requirement is the one that surprises teams running multi-region edge services: two data centers publishing the same meeting_id get no guarantee between them.
  • The key names the thing whose history matters. Here that is a meeting. In an order system it would be the order ID; in a bank ledger, the account. If the natural key is too coarse to stay under 1 MB/s, the stream probably should not be ordered at all.
  • ClearMeet keys meeting-lifecycle on meeting_id; qos-samples uses no key.

What ordering costs

  • A redelivery holds up its key: if message 2 is redelivered, message 3 waits behind it
  • The docs are explicit: ordered delivery “decreases publish availability and increases end-to-end message delivery latency”
  • With push subscriptions: one outstanding message per key at a time
  • Combined with exactly-once: acks must arrive in order, or the service fails the requests with retryable errors
Use ordering where out-of-order is wrong, not where it is merely untidy. At ClearMeet that is one topic out of four.
Speaker notes — What ordering costs
  • The availability/latency sentence is quoted nearly verbatim from docs.cloud.google.com/pubsub/docs/ordering, and it is on the slide as a quotation deliberately — it should not read as instructor opinion.
  • Discussion prompt: which of ClearMeet’s four streams need ordering? Lifecycle yes. Usage-events no (each event carries its own totals). QoS no. Media telemetry no.

The acknowledgement deadline

A delivered message is not gone from the subscription. It is set aside and hidden from other subscribers for a fixed window — the ack deadline. Ack inside the window and it is deleted. Stay silent and it goes back in the queue for someone else.
  • Default 10 s, settable 10–600 s. You rarely hit it directly: the client library watches your callback and asks for more time on your behalf until it returns
  • It stops extending at max_lease_duration — 1 hour in Python — assuming a callback running that long is stuck, not busy
The deadline exists so a crashed subscriber cannot swallow a message: the service cannot tell “still working” from “dead.”
Speaker notes — The acknowledgement deadline
  • Deadline default and range: docs.cloud.google.com/pubsub/docs/subscription-properties. max_lease_duration = 1*60*60 verified in python-pubsub types.py (FlowControl).
  • Answer the obvious question directly: acking does not "release" anything back to the queue. Ack means done — the service deletes the message from that subscription (other subscriptions on the same topic are unaffected; they hold their own copies).
  • The opposite of ack is message.nack(): give it back now rather than waiting for the deadline to lapse. Useful when your callback knows immediately it cannot handle this message and wants it retried sooner.
  • Two clocks, kept distinct: the subscription’s ack deadline is what the service enforces; the client library’s automatic extension is what keeps it from lapsing. People conflate them and set the subscription deadline to 600 s for no benefit — the library was already handling it. What raising it does buy you: fewer extension RPCs, and a longer window before a genuinely dead process is noticed.
  • subscription/expired_ack_deadlines_count (section 4) is how you see deadlines lapsing in production.

Flow control: bounding what the client holds

A pull subscription does not fetch one message at a time. subscribe() opens a long-lived StreamingPull connection, and while it is open the server sends messages down it as they become available. So the client needs its own brake.
flow = pubsub_v1.types.FlowControl(
    max_messages=1000,       # default
    max_bytes=100*1024*1024, # 100 MiB
)
streaming = subscriber.subscribe(
    subscription_path,
    callback=callback,
    flow_control=flow,
)
  • Outstanding = handed to your callback and not yet acked
  • ClearMeet’s dashboard: 1,000 × 350 B is nothing. A job holding 5 MB per message would reach the 100 MiB byte cap at ~20 messages — and that cap, not the count, is what stops it
Speaker notes — Flow control
  • Mechanism verified in python-pubsub, google/cloud/pubsub_v1/subscriber/_protocol/streaming_pull_manager.py. load is the max of (outstanding messages / max_messages) and (outstanding bytes / max_bytes). At load >= 1.0 the manager pauses the consumer; it resumes below 0.8. The gap prevents thrashing.
  • Two layers worth separating: messages arriving off the stream can sit on an internal on-hold queue inside the library, and are released to your callback as capacity frees. So “outstanding” means delivered to your code, not merely received by the process.
  • Defaults max_messages=1000, max_bytes=100 MiB from pubsub_v1/types.py. Note the docs’ own sample uses FlowControl(max_messages=10) — a deliberately tiny value to make the behavior visible in a demo.
  • If asked “would a client really take more than it can handle?” — the client is not requesting messages one at a time; the server pushes down an open stream, and the defaults are generous. It bites with large messages (the byte cap is the real limit), with slow callbacks where 1,000 held messages all need their deadlines extended while your code works through them one at a time, and on restart into a large backlog.
  • Also true and worth saying: the server applies its own limits. Docs note that if the throughput quota is exceeded the server stops sending without breaking the connection, and that Pub/Sub periodically closes and the library transparently reopens the stream.
  • Messages the client does not hold are not lost — they stay in the subscription backlog, which is durable and is where they should wait.

Speaker notes — Three ways the same event arrives twice
  • Definitions quoted from docs.cloud.google.com/pubsub/docs/exactly-once-delivery. Redelivery: “can happen either because of client-initiated negative acknowledgment of a message or when the client doesn’t extend the acknowledgment deadline of the message before the acknowledgment deadline expires.” Duplicate: “when a message is re-sent after a successful acknowledgment or before acknowledgment deadline expiration.” And explicitly: “Redeliveries are considered valid and system working as intended.”
  • The distinguishing variable is whether the message was still validly outstanding. Outside that window, a re-send is expected. Inside it — or after you already acked — it is an anomaly.
  • Be honest about the limit of our knowledge: the documentation describes what a duplicate is and that exactly-once prevents it, but does not publish why duplicates occur internally. Do not invent a mechanism. If a student asks, say the causes are not publicly documented.
  • Row 3 is the one no delivery setting can see, because from Pub/Sub’s perspective nothing went wrong: two distinct messages, each delivered correctly. That is why an application-level event key remains necessary even with exactly-once enabled.
  • Teaching order is deliberate: rows 1 and 3 are handled by idempotent consumers, which is the general, portable, free solution. Row 2 is the narrow case a product feature addresses.

Exactly-once delivery

ClearMeet’s invoicing job adds billable minutes to a running total. It cannot key the write on an event ID without a schema change, so a re-send after it already acked becomes an overcharge. This is the subscription that turns exactly-once on.
# --enable-exactly-once-delivery
def callback(message):
    add_billable_minutes(message)
    fut = message.ack_with_response()
    try:
        fut.result()
    except AckError:
        # ack failed; it is coming
        # back — undo the write
        compensate()
Without this feature ack() is fire-and-forget — you never learn it failed. Here the future resolves only when the service has recorded the ack, so your code can react.
Guarantees
  • No re-send after an ack the service confirmed
  • No re-send while the message is still validly outstanding
  • Only the newest ack ID can ack a message, so a stale one cannot
Does not cover
  • Redeliveries — a lapsed deadline or a nack() still returns the message
  • Push and export subscriptions — pull only
  • Subscribers spread across more than one region
  • Publish-side duplicates — different messages entirely
Speaker notes — Exactly-once delivery
  • Every claim from docs.cloud.google.com/pubsub/docs/exactly-once-delivery. Note the docs’ own wording: “Subscriptions with exactly-once delivery enabled don’t receive duplicate deliveries” — duplicate, not redelivery. The distinction from the previous slide is load-bearing here.
  • Say the headline plainly: this does not stop your message coming back if you were too slow. That is a redelivery and it remains expected behavior. Students who enable exactly-once and still see repeats have usually hit exactly this.
  • What ack_with_response() buys is the thing plain ack() cannot give you: confirmation. On a subscription without exactly-once, the returned future resolves immediately regardless of outcome.
  • Combined with ordering, the service expects acks in order; out-of-order acks fail with retryable errors.
  • Costs: added latency and a lower throughput ceiling per subscription. Right for usage-events; wrong for qos-samples, where the volume is high and an idempotent write is easy.

Filters: pay attention to where they run

gcloud pubsub subscriptions create qos-web-alerts-sub \
    --topic=qos-samples \
    --message-filter='attributes.device = "web" AND attributes.region = "us-east1"'
  • The filter runs in the service; non-matching messages are never delivered to that subscription
  • Filters read attributes only — never the payload. Another reason attributes matter
  • Set at creation; cannot be changed afterward
A filter is also a backlog control: filtered-out messages cannot contribute to this subscription’s backlog or its ack load.
Speaker notes — Filters
  • Verify before delivery: whether filtered-out messages are billed as delivery on that subscription. Do not claim a cost saving on the slide until confirmed — the operational benefit stands either way.
  • Immutability after creation is now a pattern (ordering, exactly-once, filter): planning the subscription matters more than planning the topic.

Reading a backlog

  • expired_ack_deadlines_count — how often deadlines lapse before an ack

Alert on oldest unacked age, not on count. Count scales with traffic; age scales with your problem.

Speaker notes — Reading a backlog

Speaker notes — The diagnosis table
  • The highlighted row is the one people misdiagnose most: near-zero backlog with a climbing age means throughput is fine and one message keeps failing. Scaling out does nothing; a dead-letter topic fixes it. Next slide.
  • Be precise about what “stuck” costs on an unordered subscription: the failing message is redelivered on a loop while every other message flows past it normally. Nothing is blocked — but the oldest-age metric climbs forever, alerts fire, and the message will silently expire at the retention limit if nobody intervenes. On an ordered subscription it is worse: everything sharing that ordering key waits behind it.
  • Rows 1 and 2 look identical in the first two columns and are opposite problems. Row 1: each message is handled promptly, there are just not enough handlers — add instances. Row 2: adding instances may not help, because each message is individually too slow and the redelivered work multiplies the load.
  • Push errors: group subscription/push_request_count by response_code; a retryable-error background rate under 1% is normal per the monitoring docs.
  • Read the three columns in order — count, then age, then expired acks. Three numbers, three different problems, three different fixes.

Retry and dead-letter

gcloud pubsub topics create qos-samples-dlq
gcloud pubsub subscriptions create \
    qos-samples-dlq-sub --topic=qos-samples-dlq

gcloud pubsub subscriptions update \
    qos-dashboard-sub \
    --dead-letter-topic=qos-samples-dlq \
    --max-delivery-attempts=5
  • A dead-letter topic is an ordinary topic you nominate. After --max-delivery-attempts failures (5–100, default 5) Pub/Sub republishes the message there and deletes it from this subscription — the pipeline is unblocked, the message is not lost
  • Nothing consumes it automatically. You write a second consumer on the DLQ’s subscription, and it must handle input the first one choked on: log, store, alert, or repair and republish
  • Retry policy governs attempts before that: immediate (default) or exponential backoff up to 600 s
Two things make a DLQ silently useless: no subscription on the DLQ topic (the same rule as any topic — with no subscription attached, the dead letters are dropped on arrival), and missing IAM: the Pub/Sub service account needs publish on the DLQ topic and subscriber on the source subscription.
Speaker notes — Retry and dead-letter
  • Range 5–100 / default 5, and retry backoff max 600 s: docs.cloud.google.com/pubsub/docs/subscription-properties.
  • The example: audio_mos is the 1–5 mean-opinion score from the BigQuery module, declared FLOAT in the table and in the topic schema. SDK 3.x serializes it as the string "four", so the consumer throws on every attempt. No number of retries fixes a message that is wrong.
  • Shape of this failure at ClearMeet: a bad client SDK release puts a steady trickle into the DLQ, and the DLQ’s own backlog metric is what tells you a release is broken.
  • What the second consumer usually does here: write the payload plus the error attribute to a table, alert if the rate crosses a threshold, and — once the SDK bug is understood — run a repair job that coerces the field and republishes to the original topic. That last step is why the DLQ consumer often needs more tolerance than the main one, not less.
  • The service account is service-<project-number>@gcp-sa-pubsub.iam.gserviceaccount.com; the console offers to grant both roles when you attach a DLQ — scripted setups must do it explicitly.

Undoing a bad deploy with seek

14:10 — ClearMeet ships a dashboard build that computes the call-health score wrong. 14:55 — it is caught. Forty-five minutes of samples were consumed and acked, and the numbers built from them are garbage. The messages are still in the subscription.
roll back the code first, then rewind the subscriptiongcloud pubsub subscriptions seek qos-dashboard-sub --time=2026-07-28T14:10:00Z
  • The cursor is the subscription’s bookmark. seek moves it: backward to re-deliver, forward to abandon a backlog you have decided not to process
  • Replaying acked messages only works if the subscription was created with --retain-acked-messages. Otherwise acked means deleted, and the rewind reaches nothing
  • Either way you cannot reach past the retention window: 7 days by default, settable 10 min–31 days
Both settings must be in place before the incident. This is a decision you make on a calm day, not during one.
Speaker notes — Undoing a bad deploy with seek
  • Retention default and range: docs.cloud.google.com/pubsub/docs/subscription-properties.
  • Order of operations matters and students get it wrong: roll back the code first, then seek. Seeking while the bad build is still running just re-processes the messages through the same bug.
  • Three distinct retention settings, worth separating explicitly: (1) unacked message retention on the subscription, the default 7 days; (2) --retain-acked-messages, which keeps acked messages inside that window so a rewind can reach them; (3) topic retention, a separate setting whose payoff is that a brand-new subscription can be seeked back to before it existed.
  • The consumer must tolerate re-processing — the same idempotency requirement from the redelivery slide, now deliberately invoked.

Snapshots: a save point before you risk it

Next Tuesday ClearMeet wants to deploy a rewritten dashboard consumer. If it is wrong they want to go back to exactly this moment — but seek --time needs a timestamp they would have to guess, and it is blunt.
before the deploygcloud pubsub snapshots create \
  pre-rewrite-snap \
  --subscription=qos-dashboard-sub

deploy, watch, decide it is wronggcloud pubsub subscriptions seek \
  qos-dashboard-sub \
  --snapshot=pre-rewrite-snap
  • A snapshot records which messages this subscription had acked at the instant you took it — the position of the bookmark, not a copy of the data
  • Seeking to it restores that position: everything the new consumer acked in between is unacked again and redelivered
Snapshot before a risky consumer change; delete it when you are confident. It is cheap insurance that does not require you to have written down a timestamp.
Speaker notes — Snapshots
  • Keep the distinction sharp: a snapshot holds ack state — which messages this subscription still owes work on. It is not a copy of the messages, and it does not extend how long messages live beyond retention.
  • Why not just seek --time? You would have to know the exact instant the deploy started, and clock skew or a slow rollout makes that guesswork. The snapshot is an explicit, named marker taken at a moment you chose.
  • Quota is 5,000 snapshots per project; they are not free to keep indefinitely, so clean up after a successful deploy.
  • Snapshots can also be applied to a different subscription on the same topic — useful for testing a new consumer against exactly the backlog the production one faced.

What it costs — and why fan-out is a design decision

Every message: billed in once, billed out four times. (illustrative topology; rates on the pricing page)
  • Billing is on throughput: publish, plus delivery per subscription
  • Four subscribers on qos-samples ⇒ every message is delivered four times and billed four times
  • Export subscriptions (BigQuery, Cloud Storage) bill at a higher per-TiB rate than standard delivery
  • Retained backlog is storage, billed separately — a stuck subscription costs money as well as freshness
A new subscriber is one command and no code. It is also a permanent increase in your delivery bill. Both facts are true; design accordingly.
Speaker notes — What it costs
  • Deliberately no dollar figures on the slide. Before delivery, read cloud.google.com/pubsub/pricing by hand and decide whether to speak numbers: the per-TiB rates, the free tier, and critically whether a minimum billable message size applies — at ClearMeet’s ~350-byte messages a 1 KB floor roughly triples the arithmetic and would change the design advice.
  • The structure is the lesson either way: one in, N out, N chosen by you.
  • If you do the arithmetic live: 1.9 TB/day published × (1 publish + 4 deliveries) ≈ 9.5 TB/day of billed throughput before any minimum-size effects.

Speaker notes — Why ClearMeet has Kafka
  • This reframes the source module’s abstract comparison as a migration-and-integration question, which is how it actually shows up in practice.
  • Per the brief: Pub/Sub is the approved product; Managed Kafka may also get approved. Students should leave knowing what they could do with it and roughly how.

What Google manages, what you configure

  • You set two numbers — total vCPU and total RAM. Google decides how many broker VMs that becomes, creates them, and replaces them when they fail
  • You never size or attach disks: at least 100 GiB of SSD per vCPU is provisioned automatically, and older segments move to tiered storage
  • When brokers are added or lost, partition rebalancing — moving replicas so load is even — happens without a reassignment tool
  • Clusters are three-zone with equal resources per zone; no single- or two-zone option, and you cannot pick the zones
  • Runs Apache Kafka 3.7.1; minor versions are not auto-upgraded
  • Auth is SASL or mTLS; unauthenticated connections are refused
The first real difference from Pub/Sub: there is a size to choose — and therefore a size to get wrong.
Not serverless — provisioned, with the operations removed.
Speaker notes — What Google manages
  • All claims from docs.cloud.google.com/managed-service-for-apache-kafka/docs/overview. The pinned Kafka version (3.7.1 at time of writing) is worth stating precisely — portability arguments depend on it. Re-verify before delivery.
  • Default quotas per project per region: 5 clusters, 750 vCPU, 6,000 GiB memory, 200 TiB broker disk (service limits, adjustable by request).
  • Pricing shape (vCPU-hour + RAM + storage) could not be confirmed by automated fetch — check cloud.google.com/managed-service-for-apache-kafka/pricing by hand before quoting anything.

Where the two models actually differ

ClearMeet’s ML team wants to retrain the call-drop model on the last 30 days of media telemetry, while the live anti-cheat consumer keeps running untouched. How each system answers that request is the difference.
Neither answer is wrong. Kafka keeps history and lets clients navigate it; Pub/Sub keeps a work queue per consumer and expects history to live in your warehouse. Most of the remaining differences follow from this one.
Speaker notes — Where the two models actually differ
  • If a student takes one idea from this section, this is it. The vocabulary slide next is entirely consequences of it.
  • Define offset out loud when the diagram appears: every record appended to a partition gets the next integer, permanently. “Offset 2” identifies a position in the log the same way a page number identifies a place in a book — and like a bookmark, several readers can hold different ones.
  • Be fair to Pub/Sub here: it is not that replay is impossible, it is that replay is bounded by a retention window you configured in advance and is done by moving one subscription’s cursor, not by many consumers independently choosing positions. The ClearMeet answer in practice is the one on the right — the 30 days already landed in BigQuery via the export subscription, which is a perfectly good place to retrain from.
  • The cost shape differs too: Kafka pays to store the history on cluster disks; Pub/Sub pays per delivery and pushes storage to BigQuery or Cloud Storage.

What actually forces the choice

Choose Managed Kafka when…
  • You have Kafka code, Connect connectors, or Streams/ksqlDB jobs you are not rewriting
  • You need partition-level ordering with parallelism — many consumers, strict order within each partition
  • Consumers must control their own offsets and replay arbitrarily
  • You need API portability across clouds or on-prem
Choose Pub/Sub when…
  • You are writing new code on Google Cloud
  • You want no capacity decision at all
  • You need push delivery, direct-to-BigQuery export, or per-subscription filtering
  • Traffic is spiky and you do not want to size for the peak
Scale is on neither list. Both handle high volume. Choose on model, ecosystem, and who operates it.
Speaker notes — What forces Kafka
  • The source module’s answer is “operational simplicity vs. API portability” said five ways. These are the concrete requirements behind the slogan.
  • ClearMeet lands on both: Pub/Sub for the cloud-native streams, Managed Kafka for the media-server telemetry the telephony team already owns. That is the normal outcome, not a compromise.

Bridging the two

ClearMeet needs media telemetry in qos_events alongside everything else, and the chat stream needs to reach the legacy toxicity engine that only speaks Kafka. Neither team is rewriting its consumers.
  • Kafka Connect is Kafka’s standard plugin runtime for moving data in and out, and the managed service runs it for you. Two directions, named from Kafka’s point of view: a sink connector takes records out of Kafka into Pub/Sub; a source connector pulls from Pub/Sub into a Kafka topic
  • ClearMeet uses both — sink for media telemetry heading to BigQuery, source for chat heading to the toxicity engine
  • Once media telemetry lands in a Pub/Sub topic it is ordinary: the same subscription types, DLQ, and metrics apply
Bridging is normal, not a transition state. Plenty of architectures keep both permanently, each stream on the side that fits it.

Recap

  • A subscription is a queue with a cursor; fan-out means independent queues, independent backlogs, independent bills
  • Delivery is provisional until you ack. A redelivery after a lapsed deadline is expected behavior, not a fault — idempotent processing is the general answer, and exactly-once addresses only the narrower “duplicate” case
  • Ordering, exactly-once, and filters are fixed at subscription creation
  • Two metrics diagnose almost everything: unacked count and oldest unacked age
  • Kafka is a log with consumer-held offsets; Pub/Sub is a queue with a server-held cursor. Choose on model and ecosystem — never on scale
