Launching a job: two payloads, two destinations

Scenario: you run python3 pipeline.py --runner=DataflowRunner …. Before anything executes in the cloud, the SDK does exactly two things.

The service receives the graph; the workers fetch the code. The Dataflow service never touches your staged artifacts — and the worker VMs are deleted when the job completes, whether it succeeds or fails.
Speaker notes — Two payloads, two destinations
  • Precision matters here because the IAM half depends on it. Older materials say "the SDK uploads your code and sends it to the Dataflow service" — that blurs two different payloads with two different permission checks:
    • Payload 1: an API call — projects.locations.jobs.create. The request body is a Job resource: its steps field is the execution graph (built locally, serialized to JSON) and its environment field carries region, machine type, staging/temp locations, and the worker service account. Gated by dataflow.jobs.create.
    • Payload 2: the code artifacts (JARs, Python dependencies), uploaded to the Cloud Storage staging location. Gated by storage permissions. The workers download these at VM startup; the service never reads them.
  • One launch therefore touches up to three permission surfaces: the Dataflow API call, the staging upload, and (coming in Section 2) actAs on the worker service account named in environment. That's the whole IAM half in one sentence.
  • Evidence students may meet in the wild: the "job graph too large" launch error — the --experiments=upload_graph workaround moves the graph out of the request body into GCS, which only makes sense once you know the graph normally travels in the API request.
  • The two-box split (Google-managed project vs. your project) is the same split Module 4 builds on for data locality — worth pointing at it now.

One launch, three identities

Every stage of the launch runs as a different credential — and each is checked independently, so a launch can fail at any of the three.

This is the map for the next three sections — one credential at a time, in launch order.
Speaker notes — One launch, three identities
  • This is the anchor slide of the module — everything in the IAM half is a zoom-in on one of these three boxes. Say the sequence out loud: you submit, the Dataflow service agent builds and manages the infrastructure, the worker service account runs the code and touches your data.
  • Stress "checked independently." Passing check 1 says nothing about check 3: you can be fully entitled to submit a job whose workers then can't read the source table. Different identity, different policy, different failure.
  • Useful mental shorthand to plant now and repeat all module: the worker SA is the identity your data sees. Sources and sinks never see you.
  • Vocabulary: "service agent" and "worker service account" are the current documented names. Students with older materials will know box 3 as the "controller service account" — flag the rename once here and again in Section 4.

Speaker notes — Section 2: Your User Roles
  • Arc of the section: the three grantable roles as personas (watcher, operator, launcher) → what a launch actually requires → why Developer alone can't launch → the actAs requirement that completes the production launcher.
  • The teaser in the subtitle is iam.serviceAccounts.actAs — the permission most demo projects hide (Owner/Editor include it) and most production projects trip over.

Scenario: three people, three roles

A data engineering team runs pipelines in one project. Each person gets the role that matches their job function.

PersonRoleCanCannot
Sam
auditor		 roles/dataflow.viewer See jobs, job messages, and metrics — read-only on all Dataflow resources Submit, update, or cancel anything
Dana
on-call operator		 roles/dataflow.developer Everything Sam can, plus cancel, drain, and update running jobs Launch new jobs (next slide shows why)
Priya
pipeline author		 roles/dataflow.admin The minimum set to create and manage jobs: Dataflow API + staging bucket access Impersonate a worker service account — that's a separate grant
Map roles to job functions, not seniority. The on-call person who answers the pager needs job control (Developer) — not read access to the data (which none of these roles grant).
Speaker notes — Three people, three roles
  • Two more Dataflow roles exist that are not for humans, and students should never confuse them with these three: roles/dataflow.worker (for the worker service account, Section 4) and roles/dataflow.serviceAgent (Google-managed only — the docs warn never to grant service agent roles to ordinary principals).
  • What Developer is actually for — the role name misleads. People who develop pipelines need to launch them (that's Admin). Developer fits the operator: whoever answers the pager can stop, cancel, drain, or update a job, and gets job messages and metrics — while holding no read access to the staging or data buckets. Job control without data access.
  • Honest scoping: drain, in-place update, and snapshots are streaming-only operations. A batch job just runs to completion — the only lifecycle action is cancel. In a batch-only shop, Developer-as-operator collapses to "can cancel jobs."
  • One thing no Dataflow role buys: per-job separation. All Dataflow roles are grantable only at project level — any Developer in the project can cancel any job in it. Real separation between teams means separate projects.
  • Why these gaps never show up in class demos: project Owner/Editor are supersets of all of this (including impersonation). The distinctions on this slide only bite in locked-down, least-privilege projects — which is exactly what production looks like.

What a launch actually needs: three gates

Section 1 showed the launch touching three surfaces. Each is a separate permission check on you, the submitter.

Speaker notes — Three gates
  • Nuance students trip on: Developer does hold dataflow.jobs.create (it's in the role's dataflow.jobs.* bundle). "Developer can't create jobs" doesn't mean the API refuses the call — it means a real launch needs more than that one call. The actual diff (Admin minus Developer) is small: the four storage permissions on the slide plus compute.machineTypes.get.
  • Outdated rationale to retire: older decks say Developer can't launch because it "can't view Compute Engine quota." Quota viewing rides on compute.projects.get (+ regions/zones list), and the current Developer role includes all three. Today the substantive gap is staging access, full stop. When a slide and the live permission list disagree, the permission list wins.
  • Gate 3 is scoped precisely: the docs require actAs in the user-managed worker SA section; the default-SA path imposes no such requirement (verified empirically: a launcher holding only roles/dataflow.admin, with actAs absence confirmed via Policy Troubleshooter, launches fine on the default compute SA).
  • Since no Dataflow role contains any iam.* permission, following the user-managed-SA best practice (Section 4) always means a second grant for launchers: roles/iam.serviceAccountUser on that specific SA — per-SA, not project-wide, per IAM best practice.

Quick check: the on-call page

Scenario: 3 a.m. — a streaming job is misbehaving and burning money. Dana holds only roles/dataflow.developer. Does this work?

gcloud dataflow jobs cancel $JOB_ID --region=$REGION
Yes. Developer holds dataflow.jobs.cancel (and .updateContents for drain and in-place update). Job control is exactly what the role is for.
  • Dana can also drain the job (finish in-flight data, stop reading new data) — the gentler stop for streaming pipelines
  • What Dana still cannot do: launch a replacement job, or read the pipeline's data — cancel-and-fix-at-3-a.m. stays within job control
Speaker notes — Quick check: cancel
  • Run this as an actual question — ask for a show of hands before revealing. The instinct after the last slide is "Developer can't do anything real"; the correction is that Developer is precisely the operate role.
  • Good follow-up discussion: cancel vs. drain. Cancel stops now and discards in-flight data; drain stops the sources and lets in-flight elements finish. Drain (like update and snapshots) exists only for streaming jobs — a batch job's only lifecycle action is cancel.
  • Second follow-up if the group is sharp: "could Dana update the running job in place?" Yes on permissions (streaming only) — but a replacement job launch is a create, which walks straight into the previous slide's gates 2 and 3.

Launching as someone else: role + actAs

Scenario: the production job must run as a dedicated worker SA, etl-worker@…. Priya names it at launch — which makes her impersonate it.

python3 pipeline.py --runner=DataflowRunner \
  --project=$PROJECT_ID --region=$REGION \
  --service_account_email=etl-worker@$PROJECT_ID.iam.gserviceaccount.com
Grant Priya actAs — on this SA only, not project-wide:gcloud iam service-accounts add-iam-policy-binding \
    etl-worker@$PROJECT_ID.iam.gserviceaccount.com \
    --member="user:priya@example.com" \
    --role="roles/iam.serviceAccountUser"
  • Naming a worker SA requires iam.serviceAccounts.actAs on it — in no Dataflow role
  • So the production launcher formula is role + actAs: roles/dataflow.admin plus Service Account User on that SA
  • Omit the flag and the job runs as the Compute Engine default SA — no actAs needed, but broad access
Demo projects hide this: Owner and Editor carry impersonation permissions. The missing actAs surfaces exactly where least-privilege roles are used — production.
Speaker notes — Role + actAs
  • Why impersonation, conceptually: the job will hold and use that SA's credentials for its whole lifetime. Letting anyone attach any SA to a job would let them borrow that SA's powers — so IAM requires the launcher to be allowed to "act as" the SA first. Same rule as attaching an SA to a GCE VM or a Cloud Run service.
  • Java spelling of the flag: --serviceAccount=…. Python: --service_account_email.
  • Scope the grant per-SA (as on the slide), not project-wide — project-level Service Account User means "can impersonate every SA in the project," which quietly includes powerful ones. This is straight from the service-account best-practices docs.
  • The empirical note behind the third bullet: default-SA launches were verified to need only the Dataflow role — a launcher with only roles/dataflow.admin and demonstrably no actAs launched successfully. The docs state the actAs requirement only in the user-managed-SA section.
  • Flip side worth telling: the actAs requirement is also a control. Once the job runs as etl-worker, only people with actAs on etl-worker can launch it — switching to a user-managed SA is how you narrow who can launch production jobs.
  • If asked whether Admin is required: no — the security-and-permissions docs also compose an equivalent launcher from Developer plus roles/storage.objectAdmin scoped to the staging bucket plus the same Service Account User grant. Worth reaching for when the launcher shouldn't hold read access to every bucket in a shared project (Admin grants that), and for CI/CD launcher service accounts; in a workload-scoped project, plain Admin over-grants little.

Launch pathUser rolesService agentWorker SAQuotas
Speaker notes — Section 3: The Dataflow Service Agent
  • Shortest section on purpose — the whole lesson is "know it exists, recognize its failure mode, and leave it alone."
  • "Service agent" is the current umbrella term for Google-managed per-service accounts; every GCP service that acts in your project has one. Dataflow's is simply the one this course cares about.

The Dataflow service agent

  • Created and managed by Google; assigned to your project automatically the first time you run a Dataflow job. Holds roles/dataflow.serviceAgent
  • It is the credential behind stage 2 of the launch:
    • checks your project's Compute Engine quota
    • creates and deletes the worker VMs
    • manages the job while it runs
  • You never authenticate as it, and it lives in a Google-managed project — not yours to configure
service-PROJECT_NUMBER@dataflow-service-producer-prod
    .iam.gserviceaccount.com
Hands off — twice over.
Don't modify its default grants: revoke its role and jobs stop launching (VM creation and quota checks fail).

And never grant roles/dataflow.serviceAgent to users or your own service accounts — it exists only for the agent.
Diagnostic value: if a launch fails while creating workers or checking quota — and user roles check out — the service agent's role is the next thing to inspect.
Speaker notes — The Dataflow service agent
  • Place it on the anchor diagram: this is credential 2 — the arrow from the Dataflow box into your project. It acts on your project (creating VMs) without being of your project.
  • The failure-mode discussion is worth two minutes: an org-policy sweep or an overzealous "clean up unused IAM bindings" pass that strips the agent's role produces launches that fail at VM creation with compute errors — baffling, because nobody's personal permissions changed. The docs explicitly recommend not modifying its default grants.
  • Terminology: current docs and the console say service agent. Older materials called this the "Dataflow service account" — ambiguous, since the worker SA is also loosely called that. Teaching "agent" vs. "worker SA" keeps the two unambiguous for the rest of the module.

Speaker notes — Section 4: The Worker Service Account
  • Naming, once and clearly: current docs say worker service account. The legacy name — still in older decks and even a Module 4 slide — is "controller service account." Same account. Teach "worker SA" so students can find it in today's docs.
  • Arc: what it is and the default → the production posture (dedicated SA, least privilege) → where its failures surface (mid-job, not at launch) → the troubleshooting map that closes the IAM half.

The identity your data sees

Every worker VM runs as the worker service account. All reads and writes your pipeline performs are authorized against its roles — not yours.

The default: the Compute Engine default SA, PROJECT_NUMBER-compute@developer.gserviceaccount.com — broad project access. Easy for demos; not what you want running production data.
Speaker notes — The identity your data sees
  • This is the module's most practically useful sentence: sources and sinks authorize the worker SA, not the human who launched the job. A pipeline Priya can launch may still fail to read a table Priya can read — and vice versa.
  • The default (Compute Engine default SA) is auto-created when the Compute Engine API is enabled and historically carries broad project access — which is exactly why demos "just work" and why production shouldn't use it.
  • Legacy name reminder: older materials call this the controller service account. Same thing.
  • If asked: a worker SA from a different project is possible but gated — both service agents (Dataflow and Compute Engine) in the job's project need Token Creator + Service Account User in the SA's project, and the iam.disableCrossProjectServiceAccountUsage org constraint must not be enforced. Know it exists; don't teach the mechanics.

Speaker notes — A dedicated worker SA
  • Read the table as a recipe: roles/dataflow.worker is the floor ("permissions necessary for a Compute Engine service account to execute work units") — then one role per source/sink, scoped as tightly as the resource allows.
  • Add roles/dataflow.admin to a worker SA only if the pipeline itself creates or inspects Dataflow jobs — e.g. template / Flex Template launcher patterns. Don't hand it out by default.
  • Java flag spelling again: --serviceAccount. Same semantics.
  • Tie back to Section 2: naming this SA at launch is what triggered the actAs requirement on the launcher. The two sections are one design: the worker SA defines what the pipeline can touch; actAs defines who can wield the worker SA.

Quotas

Dataflow has almost no quota of its own — it spends your project's regional Compute Engine quota: CPUs, in-use IP addresses, and persistent disks. Every launch must clear all three.
Launch pathUser rolesService agentWorker SAQuotas
Speaker notes — Section 5: Quotas
  • Connect to Section 3: the identity that performs the quota check at launch is the Dataflow service agent. The IAM and quota halves meet in that one moment.
  • Arc: where quota comes from → the three quotas one at a time (CPUs, IPs, disks) → sizing a job against all three at once.

Where Dataflow's capacity comes from

  • Workers are ordinary Compute Engine VMs — so a job consumes Compute Engine regional quota
  • The pool is shared: Dataproc clusters, GKE nodes, notebooks, and plain VMs all draw from the same regional limits
  • Checked at launch by the service agent — and it caps how far autoscaling can grow
  • Dataflow's own quota is thin: 25 concurrent jobs per project by default, plus API request rates
Other workloads in the region compete with your pipelines.
View and request increases on the console's Quotas page (CPU metric: compute.googleapis.com/cpus). Increases aren't instant — capacity planning belongs before launch day.
Speaker notes — Where capacity comes from
  • The one idea of the quota half: Dataflow spends shared Compute Engine quota. A busy GKE cluster in the same region genuinely shrinks the headroom your pipeline can autoscale into.
  • The 25-concurrent-jobs default rarely bites in class but does in real orchestration — a scheduler fanning out many small jobs hits it long before any CPU limit. It's a support-request increase like the rest.
  • "Checked at launch, constrains autoscaling": the launch check is done by the service agent (Section 3); after that, quota is the hard ceiling autoscaling saturates against — foreshadowing the sizing slide.
CPUs

CPU quota: workers × machine size

Scenario: your region has a 600 CPU quota. The job requests 100 workers.

Machine typeWorkersvCPUs eachCPUs consumedvs. 600 quota
n1-standard-11001100✓ launches
n1-standard-81008800✗ fails at launch
  • CPUs consumed = workers × vCPUs per machine type — the machine type multiplies quietly
  • Autoscaling makes it worse: budget for --max_num_workers × machine size, not the starting worker count
A job that launches fine at 10 workers can still starve at scale: quota is the ceiling autoscaling hits, months after the launch test passed.
Speaker notes — CPU quota
  • The arithmetic is trivial — the teaching point is which numbers to multiply: the autoscaling maximum, not the initial count; and the machine type's vCPUs, which people forget when they bump --machine_type for memory reasons and octuple their CPU draw.
  • Where to look: IAM & Admin → Quotas, metric compute.googleapis.com/cpus, filtered to the job's region. Same page handles increase requests.
  • Failure shape: the launch error is explicit about the quota exceeded; the subtler case is a running job pinned below its max_num_workers because the region has no headroom left — the job doesn't fail, it just stops scaling.
In-use IPs

In-use IP addresses: the quota you forgot

  • Counts VMs with external IP addresses, per region, shared across products
  • Independent of machine size: 150 one-vCPU workers and 150 eight-vCPU workers both consume 150 IPs
  • Default: Dataflow workers get external IPs — and spend this quota
  • Pipelines that only touch Google APIs and services don't need them:
    • --no_use_public_ips — saves quota, money, and attack surface (how-to: Module 4)
QuotaBinds at
CPUs (1-vCPU workers)600600 workers
In-use IPs575575 workers
The most restrictive quota wins. With 600 CPUs but 575 IPs, the job tops out at 575 external-IP workers — the CPU headroom is unreachable.
Internal-only workers consume zero of this quota — the whole limit disappears from the equation.
Speaker notes — In-use IP addresses
  • Full console name: In-use IP addresses; it counts external IPs only. Internal-only workers don't appear in it at all.
  • This is the quota-side argument for the Module 4 default posture: --no_use_public_ips plus Private Google Access on the subnet. Plant the flag here, teach the networking there.
  • "Most restrictive wins" generalizes to all three quotas — a launch must clear CPUs, IPs, and disks simultaneously. The sizing slide at the end of the section makes that a formula.
  • If asked why workers would need external IPs at all: reaching non-Google endpoints — third-party APIs, SaaS webhooks, external databases — without Cloud NAT. If everything the pipeline touches is a Google API, external IPs buy nothing.
Disks · batch

Persistent disks: batch jobs

  • Batch: one disk per worker (1:1), drawn from regional HDD or SSD quota
  • Today's default — Dataflow Shuffle runs shuffle in the service backend, in all supported regions:
    • workers need only boot image + logs → 25 GB disks
  • Legacy fallback — shuffle on the worker VMs: 250 GB disks
  • Overrides: --disk_size_gb, --worker_disk_type (suffix pd-standard or pd-ssd)
Teach the service-based world as the default: 25 GB is the batch number; 250 GB is the fallback story.
Speaker notes — Disks: batch
  • The defaults flipped since older materials were written: service-based Dataflow Shuffle is now the batch default in all supported regions. Decks that lead with 250 GB describe the fallback, not the norm.
  • The boot-disk framing makes the number stick: with shuffle off-worker, the disk "only needs space for the worker boot image and local logs" — hence 25 GB.
  • --worker_disk_type (Java: --workerDiskType) technically takes a full resource URL, but the part that matters is the suffix: pd-standard (HDD pool) vs. pd-ssd (SSD pool) — separate regional quotas.
  • Java flag spellings: --diskSizeGb.
Disks · streaming

Persistent disks: streaming jobs

  • Streaming deploys a fixed pool of disks — one per max_num_workers, allocated up front
    • launch with 3 workers, max 25 → 25 disks count against quota from minute one
  • Disks are redistributed as workers scale: 1 to 15 disks per worker
    • the 15:1 cap bounds scale-down: min workers ≈ max ÷ 15
  • Streaming Engine moves state to the backend → 30 GB disks
    • default for Python ≥ 2.21.0 and Go ≥ 2.33.0
    • Java: opt in with --enableStreamingEngine
  • Without it: 400 GB disks, and --max_num_workers is required
Speaker notes — Disks: streaming
  • The fixed pool is the surprise: disk quota is consumed by the ceiling, not the current size. Set --max_num_workers deliberately — an absent-minded "1000, why not" charges 1000 disks against quota for the job's lifetime (without Streaming Engine, at 400 GB each).
  • The 15:1 ratio explains a behavior students otherwise attribute to a bug: a streaming job (without Streaming Engine) with max 60 can't scale below 4 workers — each worker tops out at 15 disks.
  • Streaming Engine changed the defaults: on by default for Python ≥ 2.21.0 (Python 3) and Go ≥ 2.33.0 — so a current Python streaming job is the 30 GB small-disk story out of the box. The 400 GB fixed-pool mechanics now mainly apply to Java jobs that haven't opted in. --max_num_workers is required only without Streaming Engine (it's what enables autoscaling there); with it, optional.
  • Worker ceilings, current numbers: 2,000 workers per job by default (batch and streaming), up to 4,000 for batch with service Shuffle / streaming with Streaming Engine. The old "cannot exceed 1,000" figure is outdated — retire it.

Sizing a job against quota

effective max workers = min( CPU quota ÷ vCPUs per worker,
                             in-use IP quota,          — only if using external IPs
                             disk quota ÷ disk GB per worker,
                             per-job cap )             — 2,000 default · 4,000 with Shuffle/SE
  1. Compute the job's draw at max_num_workers, not the starting size — CPUs, IPs, disk GB
  2. Check each against the regional quota page, minus what other workloads already use
  3. The most restrictive quota wins — 575 IPs beats 600 CPUs every time
  4. Buy headroom early: quota increases are per-region requests and not instant
Cheap wins first: internal-only IPs remove one term from the formula entirely; Shuffle / Streaming Engine shrink the disk term to 25–30 GB per worker.
Speaker notes — Sizing a job
  • Run the worked example live: quota 600 CPUs, 575 IPs, 1-vCPU workers with external IPs → the job tops out at 575. Switch to internal-only IPs and the same job reaches 600. One flag, 25 more workers.
  • Don't forget the two non-Compute limits: 25 concurrent Dataflow jobs per project and the per-job worker cap (2,000 / 4,000). Orchestrators with many small jobs hit the first; genuinely huge jobs hit the second.
  • Step 2's subtlety — "minus what other workloads already use" — is the shared-pool point from the section opener coming home: your quota is not your pipeline's quota; it's the region's.
