The Dataflow Security Model

Every job involves two service accounts with very different jobs. Getting them straight now makes every role grant in this module make sense.
Security modelData localityShared VPCPrivate IPsCMEK
Speaker notes — Section 1: The Dataflow Security Model
  • Quick pulse-check: ask who can name the service account their pipeline code runs as. In most cohorts nobody can — which is exactly why this section exists before the features.
  • Two slides only: the two-account picture, then the map of the four features. Resist expanding this into an IAM re-teach — the IAM module already did that; this is just the vocabulary the role grants in Sections 3 and 5 depend on.

Two service accounts in every job

  • Dataflow service account (the service agent) — Google-managed
    • creates worker VMs, checks quota, coordinates your project with the Dataflow backend
    • service-…@dataflow-service-producer-prod
  • Worker service account — the identity your pipeline code runs as; it reads your sources and writes your sinks
    • default: the Compute Engine default service account
    • best practice: a dedicated SA with roles/dataflow.worker + least-privilege data access
Whoever launches the job needs iam.serviceAccounts.actAs on the worker SA (usually via Service Account User).
Speaker notes — Two service accounts in every job
  • Walk the diagram left to right: you submit the job; the Dataflow service agent — a Google-managed identity living with the Dataflow backend — creates worker VMs in your project and checks your quotas; those workers then run your pipeline code as the worker service account, and it's that identity that shows up in the ACLs of your buckets, datasets, and topics.
  • Full agent address for reference: service-PROJECT_NUMBER@dataflow-service-producer-prod.iam.gserviceaccount.com. It's created automatically when the Dataflow API is enabled; you never manage its key material.
  • The worker SA is where least privilege happens. The default (Compute Engine default SA, historically with Editor on the project) is exactly the wrong direction. Current best practice: a dedicated user-managed SA with roles/dataflow.worker plus only the specific source/sink roles the pipeline needs, passed at launch with --service_account_email.
  • The launch-failure students will actually hit: "Current user cannot act as service account …" — that's the missing iam.serviceAccounts.actAs permission on the worker SA (grant Service Account User on that SA). It's also the security feature: being allowed to launch jobs doesn't automatically let you borrow any identity in the project.
  • Terminology drift to warn about: older material (including earlier versions of this course) says "controller service account" for what is now called the worker service account. Same thing, current name. This matters again on the CMEK slides.

Section 2 of 5

Data Locality

Every job reports to a regional endpoint that stores its metadata. Choosing the region pins down where that metadata — and the workers — live.
Security modelData localityShared VPCPrivate IPsCMEK
Speaker notes — Section 2: Data Locality
  • Shape of the section: what the regional endpoint is → why "metadata" is a bigger deal than it sounds (the compliance slide) → how to set the region. Three slides.
  • The story to keep straight: user data stays with the workers; metadata goes to the endpoint; --region places both in the same region. Everything else in the section hangs off that sentence.

Every job reports to a regional endpoint

  • The regional endpoint is the Dataflow backend, in a Google-managed project — it deploys and controls your workers
  • It stores and processes the job metadata: what you see in the Dataflow UI
    • job name, ID, parameters, pipeline graph, start time
    • worker health checks, work-item status, autoscaling events
    • unexpected events: launch failures, unhandled exceptions from your code
  • Your data is processed by the workers in your project — it doesn't go to the endpoint
Speaker notes — Regional endpoint
  • Anchor it in something students have seen: everything in the Dataflow console UI — the job graph, step status, autoscaling chart, error messages — is a rendering of the metadata the endpoint holds. That's the concrete answer to "what is job metadata?"
  • The endpoint traffic in the arrows: worker health checks, work-item requests and status, autoscaling events, plus the unexpected events — job launch failures, work-item failures, and unhandled exceptions thrown by user code. Hold on that last item; the next slide is entirely about it.
  • Why specify a region (two headline reasons, one bonus): (1) security and compliance — data-residency regulation; (2) latency and egress cost — keep workers next to sources and sinks; (3) [docs] resilience and disaster-recovery planning — region choice is part of your DR posture.
  • Placement detail if asked: within the region, the zone is auto-selected by available capacity. Jobs using Dataflow Shuffle or Streaming Engine can have workers spread across multiple zones in the region; otherwise a single best zone is picked at job creation.

"Metadata" holds more than you'd think

  • User-authored strings: job name, pipeline option values (table names, file paths — sometimes secrets that shouldn't be there), and the pipeline graph — transform names reveal business logic
  • Error payloads: unhandled exceptions from your code are sent to the endpoint — and stack traces frequently embed actual record contents
Unhandled exception → sent to the regional endpoint:ValueError: could not parse row
'cust_id=4411, name=J. Smith, ssn=…'
Pipeline logs go to Cloud Logging, which is global by default — route them to a regional log bucket if residency matters.

Speaker notes — Metadata & compliance
  • The point of the slide: "only metadata goes to the endpoint" sounds reassuring until you enumerate what metadata contains. The terminal panel is the concrete leak path: user code throws on a bad record, the exception message interpolates the record, and now a customer row is sitting in job metadata. This is why auditors refuse to wave metadata through as harmless.
  • Secondary leak paths worth naming: pipeline option values (people pass connection strings and occasionally passwords as options — don't), and transform names (a graph reading "FilterHighRiskCustomers → ScoreCreditworthiness" reveals business logic on its own).
  • The regime table is for recognition, not memorization — students should recognize their regulator in it. The generic version: "a bank in certain countries must keep data in-country" — the table is that sentence made specific. Canada's Protected B is why northamerica-northeast1 shows up in Google's own examples.
  • The logging caveat is the one operational to-do: job metadata is regionalized by --region, but worker log messages flow to Cloud Logging, which stores them in a global bucket by default. Cloud Logging supports regional log buckets; redirect the _Default sink if residency applies. Same reasoning as the stack-trace example: log lines frequently contain record contents.
  • Defensive coding tie-in: catching parse errors and dead-lettering bad records (the pattern from the Beam concepts module) doesn't just protect throughput — it keeps record contents out of exception metadata.

Setting it: --region

One region for endpoint + workers (zone auto-selected):python3 my_pipeline.py \
  --runner=DataflowRunner \
  --project=$PROJECT_ID \
  --region=northamerica-northeast1
Pin a specific zone within that region (rare — licensing, hardware):  --region=northamerica-northeast1 \
  --worker_zone=northamerica-northeast1-b
One region, everything in it: since Beam SDK 2.44.0, workers cannot run in a different region than the job — the flag places the endpoint and the workers.
  • --region is required on current SDKs — there is no sensible default to fall back on
  • Co-locate sources, sinks, and staging buckets with the job region
    • cross-region reads and writes cost egress and add latency on every element
  • Zone is auto-selected by available capacity — don't pin one without a reason
Older examples show --worker_region putting workers in a different region than the endpoint. That split is obsolete — don't teach it, don't copy it.
Speaker notes — Setting the region
  • The mental model to leave the room with: one flag, one region, everything in it — endpoint, metadata, workers. Add --worker_zone only for a concrete constraint (zone-locked licenses, specific hardware).
  • The history, for when students hit old examples (including older versions of this course): regional endpoints originally existed in only a handful of regions (us-central1, europe-west1, a few others). --worker_region existed so you could process data in a region with no endpoint — e.g. workers in northamerica-northeast1 for Canadian residency, controlled from us-central1 — and the accompanying reassurance was "only metadata leaves the region." Two changes made that obsolete: endpoints rolled out to essentially every Compute region, and Beam SDK 2.44.0 dropped support for workers in a different region than the job. Dataflow now rejects such jobs at submission.
  • Note the previous slide's caveat still applies after this one: --region handles metadata; logs are a separate residency surface (regional log buckets).
  • The egress point deserves a number if your cohort likes numbers: inter-region egress within the same continent is billed per GiB — on a pipeline that shuffles terabytes, a misplaced staging bucket is real money, before any compliance concern.
  • Section takeaways to state before moving on: (1) region choice controls where metadata and processing live; user data stays with the workers. (2) Always set --region; co-locate storage with it. (3) Logs are the residency loophole to remember.
Section 3 of 5

Shared VPC

Organizations centralize network control in one host project and let workloads live in others. Dataflow workers can join a shared subnet like any other Compute workload — with one role grant and one flag.
Security modelData localityShared VPCPrivate IPsCMEK
Speaker notes — Section 3: Shared VPC
  • Framing: this section is less "a Dataflow feature" and more "Dataflow behaving well in an enterprise network." In any org with a networking team, the shared-VPC scenario is the normal case, not the advanced one.
  • Shape: what Shared VPC is → how to point a job at a network (and the one role grant) → the two capacity gotchas that actually break jobs in practice.

One network, many projects

  • The host project owns the VPC: subnets, routes, firewall rules — administered centrally by the network team
  • Service projects attach to it and run workloads in the shared subnets — owning their workloads without owning the network
  • A Dataflow job can run in a VPC in its own project or in the host project's — default or custom networks, either way
  • Workers get IPs from the shared subnet's range — the subnet is a real, shared resource
Speaker notes — Shared VPC concept
  • The organizational "why" before the technical "how": network controls (firewall rules, routes to on-prem, IP allocation) need one owner; workloads need many owners. Shared VPC is exactly that separation — org admins manage the network in the host project, and delegate workload management to the service projects.
  • Dataflow's part in the picture is deliberately boring: workers are just Compute Engine VMs, so they participate in Shared VPC exactly like any other VM — they attach a NIC to the shared subnet and consume its IPs. There is no Dataflow-specific networking magic; that's the reassuring message.
  • The "subnet is a real, shared resource" bullet plants the capacity slide: Dataflow workers, GKE nodes, and app VMs all draw from the same IP range, which is why the free-IP math two slides ahead matters.
  • A service project attaches to at most one host project — a detail that becomes relevant on the next slide when the --network flag has to resolve a bare network name.

Pointing a job at the network

Preferred — name the subnet with its full URL (works everywhere):  --subnetwork=https://www.googleapis.com/compute/v1\
/projects/HOST_PROJECT_ID/regions/us-central1\
/subnetworks/dataflow-subnet
Auto-mode networks in your own project only:  --network=default
When unsure, specify only --subnetwork — the network is inferred from it. Custom-mode networks and Shared VPC subnets require it; neither flag at all means the auto-mode network named default.

The one role grant

  • The Dataflow service agent (Section 1) needs Compute Network User in the host project
  • Grant at the subnet level for least privilege — project level works but shares every subnet
Without the grant, the job fails at launch — the agent literally isn't allowed to attach worker NICs to the shared subnet.
Speaker notes — Network flags & role grant
  • The current-docs guidance is the green callout, verbatim: when unsure, specify only --subnetwork. The network is inferred from the subnet. --network takes a bare name, resolves it against the job's project, and works only for auto-mode networks — it cannot select custom-mode networks, which rules out most real enterprise topologies.
  • The subtlety, if a sharp student pushes: --network can reach a host-project network — but only when that network is auto-mode and the launcher has project-level permissions on the host project. Since a service project attaches to at most one host project, a shared network name has only one candidate to resolve to. When you need to name the project explicitly — which is the normal Shared VPC case — the --subnetwork URL is the tool: the host project ID is right there in the path.
  • Older course versions paired --network with Python and --subnetwork with Java — both flags exist in both SDKs (Java spellings: --network, --subnetwork); the pairing was illustrating project-level vs. subnet-level grants, not a language difference.
  • Which identity gets Network User matters: it's the Dataflow service agent — the account that creates worker VMs and attaches their NICs — not the worker SA and not the human launcher. This is the first payoff of Section 1's two-account picture.
  • Subnet-level vs. project-level grants: subnet-level is the least-privilege option and what network teams usually prefer — the data team gets exactly one subnet, not the whole estate.

Two gotchas that break real jobs

1 · Subnet IP space caps your workers

Size the subnet for the job's autoscaling maximum (plus everything else in it) — not the initial worker count.

2 · Workers must reach each other

Allow worker-to-worker TCP 12345 (streaming) and TCP 12346 (batch). In Shared VPC these rules live in the host project — the classic "job hangs at scale-up."
Speaker notes — Capacity gotchas
  • Gotcha 1 — the IP math: each worker VM takes one address from the subnet. A /29 has 8 addresses minus reserved ones — at most 4 workers, full stop; the deck's canonical example. A /24 gives roughly 252 usable. The failure mode isn't a clean error at launch: the job starts fine at 5 workers, then autoscaling tries to go to 80 during the nightly peak and stalls against the IP ceiling — or worse, GKE grabbed half the range yesterday. Ask the network team what else shares the subnet.
  • Gotcha 2 — the firewall ports: when workers shuffle on-VM, grouped data moves directly between workers on TCP 12345 (streaming) and 12346 (batch). Default networks have a rule allowing internal traffic; custom-mode networks — which is what enterprise Shared VPCs are — typically start closed. Scope the allow rule with the dataflow network tag on both sides. And in Shared VPC, firewall rules belong to the host project, so the data team can't fix this themselves — call it out because it presents as a mystery hang at first shuffle, not as a permission error.
  • Forward reference: jobs on Dataflow Shuffle / Streaming Engine move much of this traffic into the service instead of worker-to-worker — but the firewall guidance stands; don't rely on the service backends to excuse a closed network.
  • Section takeaways: (1) Shared VPC separates network admin from workload ownership; Dataflow joins like any Compute workload. (2) Prefer --subnetwork with the full URL. (3) Network User to the Dataflow service agent, subnet-level. (4) The two capacity gotchas on this slide are the ones that actually page people.

Private IPs

By default every worker gets a public IP address. Most pipelines never need one — and turning them off removes the whole internet-facing attack surface at the cost of one flag.
Security modelData localityShared VPCPrivate IPsCMEK
Speaker notes — Section 4: Private IPs
  • Position this as the default posture to teach, not a hardening extra: a pure Google Cloud pipeline (GCS → Dataflow → BigQuery) loses nothing by dropping public IPs, and many org policies now require it.
  • Two slides: what turning off public IPs buys and costs, then the one prerequisite (Private Google Access) that makes it work — which is also the lab's networking step.

Taking workers off the internet

  • Default: each worker gets a public and a private IP
  • With public IPs off:
    • the internet can't reach workers — and workers can't reach the internet
    • reachable: the same VPC, a Shared VPC, peered VPC networks
    • admin and monitoring — console, logs, metrics — work unchanged
  • Bonus: no in-use external IP quota consumed — large jobs stop competing for it
Python / Java:  --subnetwork=… --no_use_public_ips
  --subnetwork=… --usePublicIps=false
Speaker notes — Private IPs concept
  • Both directions matter and they protect different things: inbound — no public address means no internet-facing surface to scan or exploit; outbound — compromised code can't exfiltrate to an arbitrary endpoint. One flag buys both.
  • Preempt the monitoring worry explicitly: students assume no public IP means no console/logs/metrics. All of that goes through Google's control plane, not through the workers' public addresses — admin and monitoring work unchanged.
  • The quota bonus is practical, not just tidy: in-use external IP addresses is a per-region quota, and a 500-worker batch job with public IPs eats 500 of it — a real launch-failure cause in busy projects. Private-IP jobs sidestep it entirely.
  • Note the flag rides along with a --subnetwork (or --network) flag — you're placing workers on a specific subnet and declining public addresses; the lab uses exactly this pair.
  • The green box at the bottom is deliberately a cliffhanger — "wait, how do they reach Cloud Storage without internet?" is the right question, and it's the next slide.

The prerequisite: Private Google Access

  • A subnet-level switch: VMs with only internal IPs can reach Google APIs and services over Google's network
  • Enable it on the worker subnet before launching private-IP jobs — otherwise workers can't reach Cloud Storage, BigQuery, or even their own staging bucket
  • Three documented paths to Google APIs: Private Google Access, Private Service Connect, or public IPs — the thing we just turned off
  • Genuine internet needs (external APIs, package installs): Cloud NAT — outbound only, no inbound path
gcloud compute networks subnets update dataflow-subnet \
  --region=us-central1 --enable-private-ip-google-access
The Python gotcha: with no public IPs, workers can't reach PyPI at runtime. Stage dependencies ahead of time — a custom container, or pre-staged wheels — or provide Cloud NAT. Otherwise the job fails at worker startup.
The posture to teach: internal-only workers + Private Google Access. Less attack surface, less IP quota, and for a pure Google Cloud pipeline — no functional loss at all.
Speaker notes — Private Google Access
  • Resolve the cliffhanger: Private Google Access is a per-subnet setting; with it on, requests to Google APIs from internal-only VMs route over Google's network and never touch the public internet. It's the difference between "private-IP job works perfectly" and "private-IP job can't even download its own staged code."
  • Ordering matters in practice: PGA is a prerequisite, not a follow-up — enable it on the subnet before the first private-IP launch. In the lab this is an explicit step; students who skip it meet the failure mode first-hand.
  • The Python/PyPI gotcha deserves emphasis — it's a top lab-failure cause and a top real-world one: --requirements_file normally has workers pip-install from PyPI at startup, which is exactly the internet access that no longer exists. Fixes, in order of preference: a custom container with dependencies baked in; pre-staged wheels; or Cloud NAT if the org allows it. Java jobs mostly dodge this — dependencies ship in the staged jar.
  • Cloud NAT's role, precisely: outbound-only translation for genuine external needs (third-party APIs, webhooks). It does not reopen an inbound path, so the attack-surface win survives.
  • For the fully locked-down variant (VPC Service Controls-style): point DNS for *.googleapis.com at restricted.googleapis.com and allow egress to its ranges — mention it exists, defer details.
  • Section takeaways: (1) internal-only is the default posture — no functional loss for pure Google Cloud pipelines. (2) It only works with PGA (or PSC) on the subnet. (3) Remember the Python dependency-staging consequence; Cloud NAT is the sanctioned escape hatch.
Section 5 of 5

Customer-Managed Encryption Keys

Everything Dataflow stores is already encrypted at rest. CMEK changes one thing — who owns the key — and that one thing is what "revocable access to our data" means to an auditor.
Security modelData localityShared VPCPrivate IPsCMEK
Speaker notes — Section 5: CMEK
  • Set the frame before any mechanics: CMEK is not "adding encryption" — everything is Google-key encrypted at rest by default. CMEK is about ownership and revocability: with your own KMS key in the path, disabling the key makes the job's stored data unreadable, by anyone, including Google. That's the sentence auditors are buying.
  • Shape: where job data actually lives at rest → how to turn CMEK on (and the region rule) → who needs the key → what CMEK never covers.

Where a job's data lives at rest

A running job stores data in four places. All four are encrypted by default with Google-managed keys — CMEK swaps in a Cloud KMS key that you control.

Staging bucket
Pipeline code and dependencies; temp import/export data (--temp_location)
Worker Persistent Disks
The disks attached to every worker VM
Dataflow Shuffle
Grouped / joined data in the shuffle backend (batch)
Streaming Engine
Shuffle and pipeline state in the streaming backend
What CMEK buys: not more encryption — revocability. Disable or destroy the key, and everything above becomes unreadable. Key ownership is the feature.
Speaker notes — Where job data lives
  • Walk the four cards with the "when does data land here" story: your code and dependencies are staged to the bucket at launch; each worker's OS and scratch space live on its Persistent Disk; and when the pipeline groups or joins, the shuffled data sits in the Shuffle backend (batch) or Streaming Engine (streaming) — the service-side backends from the earlier architecture material.
  • The yellow cards are the ones students forget: it's easy to CMEK the bucket and the disks mentally and miss that grouped data lives in a service backend — Dataflow's CMEK covers those too, which is precisely why it's a Dataflow feature and not just "use a CMEK bucket."
  • Key-based operations nuance [docs]: windowing, grouping, and joining use data keys — and in a CMEK job those are protected under your key as well (one historical carve-out for streaming jobs, covered two slides ahead).
  • The green callout is the section's thesis — say it plainly: default encryption already protects against stolen disks; CMEK protects against continued access. Revoke the key and the data is cryptographically gone. "Crypto-shredding" is the term of art if the cohort wants one.

Turning it on: one flag, three regions

Python:  --temp_location=gs://my-bucket/tmp/ \
  --dataflow_kms_key=projects/$PROJECT\
/locations/us-central1/keyRings/my-ring\
/cryptoKeys/dataflow-key
Java:  --tempLocation=gs://my-bucket/tmp/ \
  --dataflowKmsKey=projects/…/cryptoKeys/dataflow-key
  • One symmetric Cloud KMS key covers all four storage locations
  • Works for templates too: gcloud dataflow jobs run … --dataflow-kms-key=…
Region mismatch is the classic CMEK launch failure — check the key, the job, and the bucket before blaming IAM.
Speaker notes — Enabling CMEK
  • Mechanically this is the simplest feature in the module: create a symmetric key in Cloud KMS, pass its full resource name at launch, done. The complexity is entirely in the constraints — hence the diagram.
  • The three-region rule: KMS key location, Dataflow job region, and temp bucket region must all match. Global and multi-regional keys are explicitly unsupported. Note the pleasant alignment with Section 2: if you followed the data-locality advice — one region, everything in it — the CMEK constraint is already satisfied.
  • Version floor, if legacy SDKs come up [docs]: Beam SDK ≥ 2.13.0 for Java and Python; Go from 2.40.0.
  • The template variant matters for later modules: classic and flex template launches accept --dataflow-kms-key on gcloud dataflow jobs run, so the ops team can enforce CMEK without touching pipeline code — consistent with this module's theme that security is launch-time configuration.
  • Troubleshooting order in the callout is from the field: a CMEK job failing at launch is a region mismatch more often than a missing role — check geography first, IAM second (next slide covers the IAM half).

Speaker notes — Who needs the key
  • Payoff of Section 1's vocabulary: the grants go to the two service agents — the Google-managed identities doing infrastructure work — not to the identity running your code. Full addresses: service-PROJECT_NUMBER@dataflow-service-producer-prod.iam.gserviceaccount.com and service-PROJECT_NUMBER@compute-system.iam.gserviceaccount.com.
  • Why each needs it: the Dataflow agent runs the service-side backends (Shuffle, Streaming Engine) that hold your grouped data; the Compute agent is what actually encrypts worker Persistent Disks with your key. Neither task is performed by your pipeline code — hence the blue callout: no KMS role on the worker SA.
  • Terminology correction vs. older course material [docs]: earlier decks said to grant the role to the "Dataflow service account and the Controller Agent service account." "Controller service account" is legacy naming for today's worker service account — which needs no KMS role at all. The current docs list exactly the two agents on this slide; teach those.
  • The look-alike warning is a genuine field trap: …@compute-system (the agent) vs. PROJECT_NUMBER-compute@developer.gserviceaccount.com (the default SA) differ by a domain, and granting the KMS role to the wrong one produces the same launch failure you were trying to fix.
  • Failure mode to name: missing either grant fails the job at launch with a KMS permission error — annoying but honest. The role to grant: roles/cloudkms.cryptoKeyEncrypterDecrypter.

What CMEK never covers: job metadata

  • Job metadata stays under Google-managed encryption — always, CMEK or not
    • user-supplied: job name, pipeline option values, the pipeline graph
    • system-generated: job IDs, worker IP addresses
  • Section 2's lesson applies with new force: the surface you can't put your own key on is the surface you keep sensitive values out of
  • Hash or transform anything sensitive before it becomes a job name or option value
A job named after a customer, or an option holding a credential, sits outside your key — forever. Name jobs and pass options accordingly.
Streaming jobs created after March 7, 2024: all user data in a CMEK job — data keys included — is under your key. Older streaming jobs pick this up when restarted.
Speaker notes — What CMEK never covers
  • The boundary diagram is the takeaway: everything a job stores can be under your key; everything a job is described by cannot. The metadata list is the same one from Section 2 — the module's two halves meet here: metadata is the surface where residency (Section 2) and key ownership (this section) both fall back to "keep sensitive values out of it."
  • Concrete hygiene rules to dictate: job names come from a scheme (etl-orders-daily-20260728), never from data; credentials go in Secret Manager and get fetched by the worker SA at runtime, never passed as option values; and if an identifier must appear in an option, hash it first.
  • The March 2024 note, unpacked [docs]: CMEK is still opt-in — this change is about coverage within CMEK-enabled jobs. Historically, even with CMEK on, the data keys used by key-based operations (grouping, windowing, joining state) in streaming jobs fell outside your key and used Google-managed encryption — older material even had a careful sentence about "data keys are decrypted using your CMEK key" to describe the partial coverage. For streaming jobs created after March 7, 2024, the carve-out is gone: all user data, data keys included, is under your key. Long-running streaming jobs started before that date keep the old behavior until restarted — worth an audit if a team has year-old streaming jobs and a CMEK attestation to sign.
  • Section takeaways: (1) CMEK is about ownership and revocability, not more encryption. (2) One flag, three matching regions, regional keys only. (3) Metadata is never under your key — keep secrets out of names and options. (4) Two service agents get the KMS role; the worker SA gets none.
